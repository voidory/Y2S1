Single-file study app for NTU finals. `index.html` holds the whole app (inline module script, Dexie/IndexedDB). It is a **private, personal** project: the repository and the deployed site are not public, so course material is used for the owner's study only. Still write question text in our own words; do not paste slide text, slide figures or paper text into content.

The owner wants a critical peer: say so when something looks wrong, give a recommendation instead of a survey, and ask only about things that would change the output.

## Architecture (the parts that matter)

- Namespaces inside `index.html`: `Expr` (safe expression language), `DB`, `Content`, `Scheduler`, `Mastery`, `Review`, `Weakness`, `Evaluators`, `LF`, `IsomerEngine`, `Widgets`, `UI`. All are exposed on `window.__MASTERY_ENGINE__`; `tests/helpers/load-engine.mjs` loads them in a `vm` sandbox.
- **Modules.** `Content.MODULES` lists `{code, dir, recordKey}`. Each module has `public/content/<dir>/question-bank.json` and `skill-map.json`. `SEED_MODULES` holds module titles and weeks. The header switch sets `appState.activeModule`; `appState.nodes`, `composerNodes` and `skillMap` are always the **active module's**, so every screen works unchanged. Choice persists in the settings row `activeModule`. A new module needs: a `MODULES` entry, a `SEED_MODULES` entry, a `cm11-`-style reserved prefix for generated ids if any, content files, tests.
- **Question node (schema 3.0):** `variants[]`, each with `stem`, `inputSlot`, `evaluator`, optional `variables.sampled/derived` (values drawn fresh every time a question is served; derived values use `Expr`). Slots: choice, mapped_fields, typed_short, typed_synthesis, unordered_set, ordered_list, typed_equation, interactive_widget.
- **`Expr`** functions: sqrt, abs, log (base 10), ln, exp, pow, min, max, round, sigfig, sum, mean, sd (sample), nth(k, ...), fix(x,d) (string), sci(x,d) (returns `3.0\times10^{-34}`; put it inside `$...$`). Supports ternary, comparisons, `^`.
- **Numeric answers** (`mapped_fields_match` targets): `{type:'numeric', targetExpr, relTol|tolerance, sig, unit, paths:[{label,expr}], ecf:[{expr,label}]}`.
  - `paths` name a known wrong route when the student's number matches it; sign slips and powers of ten are named automatically.
  - `ecf` = error carried forward: expressions may use `u_<fieldKey>` (the student's own earlier numbers) and earn 0.5 credit with the label.
  - `{type:'keyword', expectedExpr, synonyms, displays}` is a pick-one decision field (field has `options` so it renders as a drop-down).
- Mastery retirement is in card time; weak spots read `errorTags` (`calc:<label>` tags come from named wrong routes).
- `Scheduler.FINAL_RECIPES` + `assembleRecipePaper` build a final-style mock paper from named nodes.
- Layout rule: no page scroll anywhere; phone step flow, bottom tab bar; `tests/e2e/layout-check.cjs` enforces it.

## Booklet: statistics

- CI: μ = x̄ ± ts/√N. Known value: t_cal = |x̄ − known| √N / s.
- Two samples: s_pooled = √[(s₁²(N₁−1) + s₂²(N₂−1)) / (N₁+N₂−2)]; t_cal = |x̄₁ − x̄₂| / s_pooled × √(N₁N₂/(N₁+N₂)). Paired: t_cal = |d̄| √n / s_d. If t_cal > t_table the results differ significantly at that confidence level.

## Booklet: chemical sensors and potentiometry (Topic 3b)
- Cations: E = const + (RT/nF) ln a = const + 2.303(RT/nF) log a = const + (0.0592/n) log a (25 °C). Anions: the same with a **minus** sign.
- Selectivity coefficient: K_ij^pot = (a_i × 10^((E₂−E₁)/s) − a_i′) / a_j^(n/x). E₁ = potential with primary ion only; E₂ with primary + interferent; a_i activity of primary ion alone, a_i′ of primary ion in the mixture, a_j of interferent in the mixture; s = calibration slope; n, x = charges of primary and interfering ions. Activities need I for each solution and Debye–Hückel.
- Lecture facts (Topic 3b): chemical sensor = selective chemistry region + transducer (electrochemical, optical, mass, heat). Potentiometry measures cell potential with no appreciable current: reference electrode + indicator electrode + meter. ISE membranes: glass, sparingly soluble inorganic salts (single crystal LaF₃; pressed powder Ag₂S), polymer-immobilised ionophore (PVC). Glass electrode: sodium silicate (Al₂O₃, Na₂O, SiO₂), 0.3–0.4 mm thick; conditioned in 0.1 M HCl for 1 h to make silanol groups (−SiO⁻H⁺); response ∝ a(H⁺); interferents are alkali metal ions; both electrodes sit in one barrel (internal 0.1 M HCl, Ag/AgCl). Cell notation: reference on the left, sensor on the right; E = E_RHS − E_LHS = E_G + E_G′ + E_R′ − (E_R + E_J); only E_G varies with pH; the junction potential is the largest error (worse when calibration and sample ionic strengths differ, or temperature changes). Junction potential: ions diffuse at different rates (H⁺ faster than Cl⁻), the dilute side becomes positive. Gas probes: gas-permeable membrane over an inner solution with an ISE: NH₃ (0.1 M NH₄Cl, pH glass; E = const − 59.2 log[NH₃]; amines interfere), CO₂ (NaHCO₃, pH glass), SO₂ (NaHSO₃, pH glass), HF (H⁺, F⁻/LaF₃), H₂S (pH 5 buffer, S²⁻/Ag₂S), HCN (KAg(CN)₂, Ag⁺/Ag). LaF₃ doped with EuF₂, F⁻ hops between vacancies (Frenkel defects), slope −59.2 mV, OH⁻ interferes. Ag₂S membrane: Ag⁺ mobile; senses S²⁻ indirectly through Ksp, slope −29.6 mV per decade of a(S²⁻). PVC membrane = polymer ~30%, plasticiser 65–70%, ionophore ~1%, salt ~1%; K⁺ electrode uses valinomycin (cavity size gives selectivity; serum K⁺ 3.5–5.0 mM vs Na⁺ 135–145 mM), NH₄NO₃ bridge keeps K⁺ from the KCl reference out of the sample. Slopes at 298 K: ±59.2 / ±29.6 / ±19.7 mV per decade for z = 1, 2, 3 (cations +, anions −); Nernstian if within about ±1–2 mV; linear range usually 10⁻⁵–10⁻¹ M with curvature below 10⁻⁵ M. Calibration: condition in 0.01 M of the ion for 1 h, then de-ionised water for 30 min; stir gently; steady when the potential changes by no more than ±0.1 mV in 60 s. ISA (ionic strength adjuster) makes γ constant so the electrode follows concentration; TISAB for fluoride: NaCl (ionic strength), sodium acetate (pH 5–6, avoids OH⁻), CDTA (complexes Al(III) and Fe(III)). K^pot classes: < 10⁻³ very weak, 10⁻³–1 intermediate, = 1 equally selective, > 1 prefers the interferent. Two-point method: 0.001 M primary ion alone (E₁), then 0.001 M primary + 0.1 M interferent (E₂); use 0.01 M primary if interference is strong. Worked example in the lecture: Ca²⁺ ISE, s = +29.6 mV, E₁ = −20.1, E₂ = −19.8 mV, 0.001 M CaCl₂ and 0.1 M NaCl → I = 0.003 and 0.103 M, γ(Ca²⁺) 0.79 and 0.40, γ(Na⁺) 0.77, a_i 7.9×10⁻⁴, a_i′ 4.0×10⁻⁴, a_j 7.7×10⁻², K = 6.9×10⁻² (intermediate).
- The lecture uses 59.2 mV; the booklet uses 0.0592 V. Treat both as equal.

## Booklet: atomic and molecular spectroscopy (Topic 4)
- E = hν = hc/λ with **h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00×10⁸ m s⁻¹**. T = I/I₀. A = −log T = log(I₀/I) = εbc.
- Stern–Volmer: Φ₀/Φ_Q = 1 + (k_q / (k_e + k_d)) [Q] (Φ₀ quantum yield without quencher, Φ_Q with it).
- Boltzmann: N_e/N₀ = (g_e/g₀) exp[−(E_e − E₀)/kT], k = 1.381×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (excited-state population in a flame, Topic 4c).
- Heisenberg: δE δt ≥ h/4π (natural line width of an atomic line).
- Topic 4b lecture (molecular fluorescence): fluorescence and absorption instruments differ in geometry and background: the emission is viewed away from the excitation beam (diagram shows 90°), with a fixed excitation wavelength and a scanned emission monochromator, and a near-zero background, so it is extremely sensitive (absorbance is a small difference between large signals). Fluorescence sensors are used for anions and dissolved oxygen. Jablonski ideas: absorption A; vibrational relaxation (R₁–R₄, < 10⁻¹⁵ s, energy to the solvent, ends in the lowest vibrational level); internal conversion IC (same spin, from the lowest vibrational level of one electronic state to a high vibrational level of a lower one, e.g. S₁ → S₀); intersystem crossing ISC (isoenergetic, between different multiplicities, e.g. S₁ → T₁); fluorescence F (S₁ → S₀, same spin, lifetime 10⁻⁹–10⁻⁴ s as the slide states); phosphorescence P (T₁ → S₀, different spin, 10⁻⁴–10² s); non-radiative quenching timescale 10⁻⁹–10⁻⁶ s. Stokes shift: emitted light has a longer wavelength than the excitation; causes are re-optimisation of the molecule's geometry in S₁ and interactions with the environment (solvent); absorption and emission spectra are approximate mirror images when vibrational spacings are similar and transition probabilities are similar. Quantum yield = photons emitted ÷ photons absorbed = molecules that fluoresce ÷ excited molecules; high for rigid molecules and many substituted aromatics (Φ_F → 1); a rise in temperature lowers it. Quenching: M + hν → M*; M* → M + hν; M* + Q → M + Q*, then Q* → Q + heat.
- Topic 4a and 4c lectures have not been supplied. From the papers: Beer–Lambert with dilution chains, %T ↔ A, cm⁻¹ → nm → Å, AAS components, absorbance vs fluorescence instrument layout. Qualitative AAS/layout items in the bank come from standard texts and should be checked against the slides.

## Booklet and lecture: chromatography (not yet in the app)
- Resolution R_S = 2[(t_R)_B − (t_R)_A]/(W_A + W_B) = 1.178[(t_R)_B − (t_R)_A]/(W½A + W½B) (booklet; the lecture writes 1.177, N with 5.52 and W = 1.699 W½, the booklet writes 1.178 and 5.55: the booklet is what is in the exam). R_S < 1.0 poor, = 1.0 partial, ≥ 1.5 baseline separation.
- t_S = t_R − t_M; k = t_S/t_M = (t_R − t_M)/t_M; α = t_S(B)/t_S(A) = k_B/k_A = K_B/K_A. N = L/H = 16 (t_R/W)² = 5.55 (t_R/W½)² ≈ 41.7 (t_r/w₀.₁)² / (A/B + 1.25). H = σ²/x.
- Purnell: R_S = (√N/4)((α − 1)/α)(k_B/(1 + k_B)). R_S ∝ √N ∝ √L (doubling L raises R_S by √2; doubling R_S needs 4× the length). The k_B/(1 + k_B) term levels off: 0.5 at k = 1, 0.83 at 5, 0.91 at 10.
- Band spreading: σ = √(2Dt); σ²_injection = σ²_detector = (ΔV)²/12; σ²_tubing = π d_t⁴ l_t u_v / (384 D); wall-coated column k = K/β, β = r/(2d_f); ln k = ΔH_vap/RT + ΔH_mix/RT + constant. Causes: multiple flow paths (eddy diffusion), longitudinal diffusion, finite equilibration time. Van Deemter H ≈ A + B/u_x + C u_x: below the optimum velocity B/u dominates, above it C u dominates, at the optimum B/u and Cu contribute equally and H is lowest (N greatest); open-tubular GC column has A ≈ 0. Peak asymmetry: tailing (some molecules retained too strongly, come out late) and fronting (overload, part of the band retained less strongly and comes out early); K = C_S/C_M; if K falls with concentration the peak tails, if K rises it fronts; the two are not exact opposites in mechanism.

## Topic 1 lecture: conventions that the answers must follow
- Absolute, relative and percent relative uncertainty: burette 12.35 ± 0.02 mL → relative 0.02/12.35 = 0.002 (1 s.f.), percent relative 0.2%.
- Significant figures: 100 (3), 1×10² (1), 0.01 (1), 0.000327 (3), 0.100327 (6), 10000.00 (7), 1.00×10⁴ (3). Add/subtract: the answer has no more decimal places than the least accurate number. Multiply/divide: no more significant figures than the least accurate number.
- Error propagation (lecture and booklet agree): add/subtract e = √Σeᵢ² (absolute); multiply/divide %e = √Σ(%eᵢ)² (percent relative). Mixed operations: do the sum or difference with absolute uncertainties first, convert to percent relative, then combine. Examples: 1.76(±0.03) − 0.59(±0.02) = 1.17(±0.04); 1.76(±0.03) × 1.89(±0.02) / 0.59(±0.02) = 5.6 (±4%) = 5.6 (±0.2); [1.76(±0.03) − 0.59(±0.02)] / 1.89(±0.02) = 0.619 (±0.020) = 0.619 (±3%); z = x + y − w with w 4.52 ± 0.02, x 2.0 ± 0.2, y 3.0 ± 0.6 cm → 0.5 (±0.6) cm.
- **Reporting rules:** keep every digit in the calculator and round only the final answer. The percent relative uncertainty goes to the nearest whole number (to 1 d.p. if under 1%: 0.76% → 0.8%, 0.08% → 0.1%); the absolute uncertainty to 1 significant figure (2 if it starts with 1 in some courses; the lecture rounds 0.036 to 0.04 and 0.633 to 0.6) and the answer to the same decimal place. Rounding: above half round up, below half round down, **exactly half rounds to the nearest even digit** (43.55 → 43.6, 43.45 → 43.4, 120.804500 → 120.804). Mean and s: one more decimal place than the data if N ≥ 10, the same decimal places as the data if N < 10 (10, 11, 14, 12, 4 → mean 10, s 4; with ten values → 10.1, 3.8).
- Errors: systematic (determinate: instrumental, method, human; consistent size and direction) vs random (indeterminate; treated statistically). Accuracy = closeness to the true value; precision = agreement between values (reproducibility). Gaussian: y = e^(−(x−μ)²/2σ²)/(σ√2π); 68.3% of values lie within ±1σ; s uses N − 1 (degrees of freedom); N → ∞ gives x̄ → μ, s → σ; smaller s = more precise.
- Worked examples usable as golden tests: carbohydrate 12.6, 11.9, 13.0, 12.7, 12.5 g/100 g → mean 12.54, s 0.40; 50% CI ±0.13 (t = 0.741, 4 df), 90% CI ±0.38. Known-value t-test (case a): sulfur in kerosene 0.112, 0.118, 0.115, 0.119 % against 0.123 % → t_cal = 4.43 > 3.18 (3 df, 95%): significant difference. Two methods (case b): test 20.10, 20.50, 18.65, 19.25, 19.40, 19.99 vs reference 18.89, 19.20, 19.00, 19.70, 19.40 → x̄ 19.648 and 19.238, s 0.673 and 0.324, s_pooled 0.546, t_cal 1.240 < 1.833 (9 df, 90%): no significant difference at 90% (at 50%, t_table 0.703, there would be). Paired (case c): d̄ = 2.491, s_d = 6.748, n = 11 → t_cal = 1.224 < 2.228 (10 df, 95%). Q-test: 0.11/0.20 = 0.55 < 0.64 (n = 5): keep. Pooled test is valid only when s is the same for both sets, "which can always be assumed in exercises and tests".
- Three t-test cases: (a) measured vs known value, (b) means of two methods on one sample measured several times (pooled), (c) paired, two methods on several different samples. **The bank has no case (a) generator and no CI at confidence levels other than 95%.**

## Topic 3a lecture: extra material beyond the booklet
- Salt effect: equilibrium positions depend on electrolyte concentration even without a common ion; the effect depends on the charges involved, and is nil for neutral species. Ionic atmosphere (a sheath of opposite-charge ions) lowers effective concentrations as I rises. Properties of γ: → 1 at very low I; = 1 for an uncharged molecule; departs further from 1 as charge grows; ions of equal charge have similar γ at a given I; γ is the same in every equilibrium the ion joins. D–H constants 0.51 and 3.3 hold for water at 298 K only; for I < 0.01 M and singly charged ions the denominator tends to 1 (−log γ = 0.51 z²√I); α ≈ 0.3 nm for most singly charged ions. Individual γ cannot be measured; use γ± = (γ_A^m γ_B^n)^(1/(m+n)).
- Ionic strength examples: 0.1 M KNO₃ → 0.1 M; 0.1 M Na₂SO₄ → 0.3 M; 0.05 M KNO₃ + 0.1 M Na₂SO₄ → 0.35 M.
- **Solubility with activities:** K_sp = [A]^m[B]^n γ_A^m γ_B^n, so concentrations satisfy [A]^m[B]^n = K_sp/(γ_A^m γ_B^n). Ba(IO₃)₂ (K_sp 1.57×10⁻⁹) in 0.033 M Mg(IO₃)₂: I = 0.099 M; γ(Ba²⁺) = 0.38, γ(IO₃⁻) = 0.77 (table at I = 0.1); concentrations only: [Ba²⁺] = 3.60×10⁻⁷ M; with activities: 1.60×10⁻⁶ M. The ionic strength is taken from the added electrolyte; the sparingly soluble salt is assumed not to contribute. Zn(OH)₂ (K_sp 3.0×10⁻¹⁶): concentrations only 4.22×10⁻⁶ M; with activities in 0.0100 M KCl 5.15×10⁻⁶ M and in 0.0167 M K₂SO₄ 6.2×10⁻⁶ M.
- Redox basics: split into half-reactions; balance atoms and charge (MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O). Cathode = reduction, anode = oxidation (in both galvanic and electrolytic cells). Galvanic (equilibrium) cell: spontaneous, electrons flow anode → cathode, the potential falls to 0 V as the reaction proceeds (Ag⁺/Cu starts at 0.412 V with the stated concentrations); electrolytic (non-equilibrium) cell needs an external potential larger than 0.412 V. Solids have unit activity. The double bar also carries a liquid junction potential.
- Potentials: electrode potential E (vs a defined reference), standard potential E° (all activities 1, 25 °C), **formal potential E°′** (ratio of oxidised to reduced forms is unity and stated substances are present at stated concentrations; approximates E° at mM levels). SHE is hypothetical (cannot be built because a = 1 cannot be made). Reference electrodes: SHE 0.000 V; SCE (½Hg₂Cl₂ + e⁻ → Hg + Cl⁻, saturated) +0.242 V vs SHE, no longer used (toxic); **Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) +0.205 V vs SHE**, the common one now.
- ΔG scales with n although E does not: the same reaction written with n = 1 or n = 2 has the same E_cell but ΔG° = −nFE° differs by a factor of 2.
- Worked examples (golden tests): ½Cu | ½Cu²⁺ (a = 0.0200) ‖ Ag⁺ (a = 0.0200) | Ag: E(Ag) = 0.799 − 0.0592 log(1/0.0200) = +0.698 V; E(Cu) = 0.337 − (0.0592/2)… written with ½Cu²⁺ so n = 1 → +0.287 V; E_cell = +0.411 V (E°_cell +0.462 V). Concentration cell Zn | Zn²⁺ (0.0010) ‖ Zn²⁺ (0.1000) | Zn: −0.8518 and −0.7926 V → E_cell = +0.0592 V. Zn | ZnSO₄ (c), PbSO₄ (sat.) | Pb (K_sp PbSO₄ 1.6×10⁻⁸; E° Zn −0.763, PbSO₄/Pb −0.350): c = 5×10⁻⁴ M gives −0.861 and −0.252, E_cell +0.609 V from concentrations; with activities I = 2×10⁻³ M, γ(Zn²⁺) 0.825, γ(SO₄²⁻) 0.820, E_cell +0.613 V. Table (ZnSO₄ c, I, E_cell from concentrations, from activities): 5.00×10⁻⁴, 2.00×10⁻³, 0.609, 0.613; 2.00×10⁻³, 8.00×10⁻³, 0.573, 0.582; 1.00×10⁻², 4.00×10⁻², 0.531, 0.550; 2.00×10⁻², 8.00×10⁻², 0.513, 0.537; 5.00×10⁻², 2.00×10⁻¹, 0.490, 0.521.

## Chromatography lectures (Efficiency and Band Spreading; HPLC/GC teaching material)
Two versions of the Efficiency slides were supplied. The newer says "either 5.52 or 5.55 is fine" for N = 16(t_R/W)² = 5.5x (t_R/W½)²; the booklet's 5.55 and 1.178 are what the exam supplies. **The two versions disagree on peak asymmetry:** the 28 Oct version says that if K *decreases* with concentration the concentrated centre moves faster and the band **tails**; the newer version says K decreasing gives **fronting** and K increasing gives **tailing**. Standard theory (Langmuir isotherm) agrees with the first. Ask the owner which one the lecturer is teaching before writing questions.
- Retention: t_R = t_M + t_S; k = t_S/t_M = (t_R − t_M)/t_M = (V_R − V_M)/V_M (volume = u_v × time at constant flow). α = t_S(B)/t_S(A) = k_B/k_A > 1; α = 1 means no separation. Peak widths: W = 4σ, W½ = 2.354σ, W = 1.699 W½, W½ = 0.589 W (W/2 ≠ W½). R_S = 2(t_B − t_A)/(W_A + W_B) = 1.177 (t_B − t_A)/(W½A + W½B) in the lecture.
- Worked examples (golden tests): A 5.0 min, B 6.0 min, widths 0.4 and 0.5 → R_S = 2.22. Limonene 8.36 min (W 0.96) and γ-terpinene 9.54 min (W 0.64) → R_S = 1.475. Baygon t_R 8.68 min, W 0.29 min → N = 14334 plates; 2.0 m column → H = 139.53 µm. α = 1.09, k_B = 3.43: N = 16 R_S² (α/(α−1))² ((1+k_B)/k_B)² = 3915 plates for R_S = 1.0, 15659 for R_S = 2.0; H = 0.2 mm → L = 783 mm for R_S = 1.0.
- Different solutes can have different H on the same column (diffusion, retention, mass transfer differ). Smaller H and larger N = more efficient. H = σ²/x; σ = √(2Dt).
- Band spreading: multiple flow paths (reduce by narrow particle size range and uniform packing), longitudinal diffusion (greater at low flow; worse in GC because diffusion in gases is faster), finite equilibration time / mass-transfer resistance (reduce by slow flow, small particles, thin films). van Deemter H ≈ A + B/u + Cu; A = 0 for an open tubular GC column. Packed columns: N ∝ L/d_p; smaller particles give a smaller A and shorter diffusion paths (smaller C) but need higher pressure (pressure ∝ η u L / d_p²).
- Tailing: some molecules retained more strongly (secondary interactions with residual silanol groups, active sites); asymmetry A/B > 1 (tailing) or < 1 (fronting) measured at 10% height, N ≈ 41.7 (t_R/w₀.₁)² / (A/B + 1.25). Fronting: column overload. Cures: reduce the injected amount, end-cap or silanize (trimethylsilyl groups block Si–OH), adjust pH or mobile phase.
- HPLC: analytical column packed with 2–10 µm silica; guard column (same packing, shorter) protects it; column label e.g. C18, 60 Å pore, 4 µm particle, 3.9 × 150 mm (length × inner diameter); ~100 Å pores for small molecules, ~300 Å for peptides and proteins. C18 and C8 are alkyl chains bonded to silica silanols; C8 is less hydrophobic and retains less. Silica is unstable at high pH. Normal phase: polar stationary phase (amino, diol), non-polar mobile phase, least polar elutes first. Reversed phase: non-polar stationary phase (C18, C8), polar mobile phase, **most polar elutes first**; raising the organic fraction (ACN, MeOH) shortens retention (eluent strength rises as the solvent gets less polar). Isocratic = fixed solvent composition; gradient = composition changes during the run to elute strongly retained solutes. Injection by loop injector. Detectors: UV (most common, lamp + monochromator, diode array, linear over five orders of magnitude), refractive index (universal, about 1000× worse detection limit than UV), electrochemical (only electroactive compounds, selective and sensitive). LC–MS is hard because MS needs gas-phase ions under high vacuum, so the liquid must be desolvated (ESI, APCI interfaces); HPLC suits polar, non-volatile, thermally labile compounds.
- GC: mobile phase He, N₂ or H₂ (inert, low viscosity); analytes gas or volatile liquid stable at 50–300 °C. Open tubular columns (WCOT wall-coated; PLOT porous layer, for O₂, N₂, CO; SCOT support-coated, intermediate) give higher resolution, shorter analysis and higher sensitivity but lower sample capacity than packed columns, which have higher capacity, broader peaks, longer retention, lower resolution. The stationary phase is bonded or cross-linked to limit bleed; thick films shield silanols (less tailing) but bleed more; like dissolves like (PDMS non-polar; 50% phenyl slightly polar). Derivatisation converts a compound to a volatile or detectable form (carboxylic acid → trimethylsilyl ester). Carrier gas: optimal flow rises N₂ < He < H₂; H₂ gives low C term but is explosive and unsuitable for MS. Temperature programming (hold, ramp 5–20 °C/min, bake-out) fixes the mix of boiling points; pressure programming has the same effect without heat. Injection: split (high concentration, avoids overload), splitless (trace, vent opened later), on-column (thermally labile or high boiling). Detectors: TCD (near-universal, non-destructive, less sensitive, helium carrier), FID (hydrogen–air flame, organic compounds, highly sensitive, destructive), MS (m/z, specific identification).
- Retention is not covered by any generator yet; all of the above is new content.

## Topic 2 lecture: air pollution in Singapore
- AQI (US EPA index, universal) vs PSI (older EPA index, replaced by AQI in the 1980s elsewhere; **Singapore's NEA is the only agency still using PSI**). Same pollutants (SO₂, NO₂, O₃, CO, PM10, PM2.5); PSI gives lower values for the same data. AQI bands: 0–50 good, 51–100 moderate, 101–150 unhealthy for sensitive groups, 151–200 unhealthy, 201–300 very unhealthy, 301+ hazardous. PSI: 0–50, 51–100, 101–200 unhealthy, 201–300 very unhealthy, 301+ hazardous.
- PSI calculation (booklet formula): the sub-index uses I_max,J, the **top of the lower band** (not its bottom), and the overall PSI is the **largest sub-index** (confirmed on Topic 2 p16). For the lowest band set the lower anchor to 0. Worked: PM2.5 70 µg/m³ lies between 55 and 150, PSI anchors 100 and 200 → I_p = (200−100)/(150−55) × (70−55) + 100 = 116; PM10 70 µg/m³ → 60; PM2.5 6 µg/m³ in the good band → (50/12) × 6 = 25; the same PM2.5 70 gives AQI 158. AQI PM2.5 bands: 0–12 good, 12.1–35.4 moderate, 35.5–55.4 sensitive, 55.5–150.4 unhealthy, 150.5–250.4 very unhealthy, >250.5–500 hazardous. The slides print µg/m³ as "mg/m³" in the text layer only; the figures show µg/m³.
- Particulate matter: aerosol = stable suspension of solid or liquid particles in a gas; PM10 and PM2.5 are defined by aerodynamic diameter ≤ 10 and ≤ 2.5 µm; composition varies regionally, seasonally and daily (organic, inorganic, biological; may carry metals or PAHs). Most Singapore PSI readings come from PM2.5.
- "Cigarette scale": resting breathing 5–8 L/min (40–60 while exercising); at 8 L/min that is 0.48 ≈ 0.5 m³ per hour, 12 m³ per day, 84 m³ per week, 4368 m³ per year (52 weeks), ≈ 350,000 m³ in 80 years. A hazardous day of 275 µg/m³ (PSI 325) → 275 × 12 = 3300 µg = 3.3 mg per day. One cigarette is estimated to contain 12 mg PM2.5, so that is 0.275 cigarettes/day (about one every 4 days). Reverse: one cigarette in 3 min ≙ 12000 µg / (8×10⁻³ m³ × 3) = 500,000 µg/m³; a pack (20) a day ≙ 20 × 12000 / (8×10⁻³ × 60 × 24) ≈ 21,000 µg/m³, about 75× the worst haze. AQI cannot say what the particles are made of; health effects at given PM levels are uncertain.
- Elemental analysis of PM: sample on a filter → microwave acid digestion (HNO₃, HCl) → filtration → dilution → ICP-MS (ppb–ppm range, some elements ppt). **Enrichment factor** EF_X = ([X]/[Al])_sample / ([X]/[Al])_crust. EF < 10 crustal origin; 10 < EF < 100 moderately enriched by anthropogenic sources; EF > 100 industrial, incineration and vehicle emissions. Haze periods raise K, V, Co, Zn, Se, Cu, Ga, Ge, As, Zr, Mo, Rh, In, Sn, Te, Cs, Hf, Os, Tl (burning vegetation or re-mobilised deposited particles); the Hungry Ghost festival (joss paper, bank notes, incense) raises many of the same elements. Platinum-group and rare-earth elements are also tracked. Unmonitored but hazardous: PAHs, VOCs, dioxins, all raised by burning.

## Topic 4a lecture: molecular absorption
- Units: 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m = 10⁻⁸ cm; 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m = 10 Å; wavenumber = 1/λ in cm⁻¹ (500 nm = 500×10⁻⁷ cm = 20000 cm⁻¹); c = νλ, c = 3.00×10⁸ m s⁻¹ = 3.00×10¹⁰ cm s⁻¹; E = hν = hc/λ with h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s: a 5.00 µm photon has E = 3.98×10⁻²⁰ J. Energy order electronic > vibrational > rotational; rotational transitions microwave to far-IR, vibrational mid and far-IR (formaldehyde symmetric C–H stretch 2785 cm⁻¹), electronic UV-vis (spectra broad because vibrational and rotational levels are superimposed).
- Beer–Lambert: T = P/P₀, A = −log T = log(P₀/P) = εbc; A dimensionless, b in cm, c in M, ε in M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Examples (golden tests): 7.25×10⁻⁵ M KMnO₄, T = 44.1%, 2.10 cm cell at 525 nm → A = 0.356, ε = 2.34×10³ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Amine picrate (359 nm, ε = 1.25×10⁴; 0.1155 g diluted to 100 mL, 1 mL → 250 mL, A = 0.454, 1.00 cm) → c = 3.632×10⁻⁵ M, ×250 = 9.08×10⁻³ M, n = 9.08×10⁻⁴ mol, M = 127 g/mol, 4-chloroaniline (127.57). Deviations from Beer's law: high concentration (molecules interact, density changes), association/dissociation/reaction of the absorber (HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, only one form absorbs), non-monochromatic light and stray light.
- **Scatchard plot** (K for P + X ⇌ PX when P and PX absorb, X does not; X measured separately, e.g. by ISE): ΔA/[X] = KΔε P₀ − KΔA (Δε = ε_PX − ε_P for b = 1 cm, P₀ total P). Plot ΔA/[X] against ΔA; slope = −K. Worked data (470 nm, 1.00 cm, A₀ = 0.226): [X] 0.030, 0.060, 0.120, 0.180, 0.240, 0.300 M → A 0.812, 1.090, 1.316, 1.406, 1.488, 1.535 → ΔA 0.586, 0.864, 1.090, 1.180, 1.262, 1.309 → ΔA/[X] 19.5, 14.4, 9.08, 6.56, 5.26, 4.36; the slide's slope is −21.5 so K = 21.5 (end points alone give −20.9, so use a least-squares fit when generating answers and keep a wide tolerance).
- Spectrometer: source, monochromator, sample cell, detector, readout (single beam and double beam). Sources: continuum (tungsten/halogen 320–2500 nm, about 3500 K; deuterium or hydrogen lamp, UV 200–400 nm via D₂ + E → D₂* → D′ + D″ + hν) and line (laser). Monochromator: entrance slit, collimating mirror, grating, focusing mirror, exit slit; spectral bandpass 1–10 nm at half height; narrow bandwidth gives higher resolution but slower scans; bandwidth should be as large as possible yet small compared with the peak width. Cuvets (glass/quartz), photomultiplier detector.

## Topic 4c lecture: atomic spectroscopy
- Three types: absorption of sharp lines from a hollow-cathode lamp (AAS); emission from a thermally populated excited state (AES); emission following initial absorption (atomic fluorescence). AES emission wavelength equals the excitation wavelength (no molecular orbitals, no Stokes shift); thermal excitation uses a plasma.
- Na 3s → 3p, 4p, 5p at **590, 330, 285 nm** (the slide text lists them in the wrong order; the figure is right; see the register).
- Hollow-cathode lamp: lines narrower than 10⁻³–10⁻² nm; high voltage ionises Ar → Ar⁺ + e⁻; Ar⁺ strikes the cathode and sputters metal atoms; they collide with energetic electrons and are excited; the excited atoms emit photons of the same frequency the analyte absorbs. Atomisation: nebuliser makes an aerosol, mixes with oxidant and fuel, baffles remove large droplets, only about 5% of the sample reaches the flame; desolvation in the primary combustion zone, vaporisation and atomisation in the hottest interzonal region, oxides and hydroxides in the outer cone; oxygen or N₂O oxidants for harder-to-atomise heavy metals.
- ICP: argon plasma, RF power 27 or 40 MHz, 6,000–10,000 K; Tesla coil spark starts ionisation; observation 15–20 mm above the induction coil where the plasma is transparent. Advantages: complete desolvation, vaporisation and atomisation; inert atmosphere; near-uniform temperature cross-section; thin optical path; many elements at once (polychromator with a grating and 48–60 exit slits and detectors).
- Boltzmann: N_e/N₀ = (g_e/g₀) exp[−(E_e − E₀)/kT]. Worked: Na lowest excited state 3.371×10⁻¹⁹ J, g_e/g₀ = 2: 1.67×10⁻⁴ at 2600 K (less than 0.02% excited), 1.74×10⁻⁴ at 2610 K, a **4% rise for a 10 K change**. Small temperature changes barely change the ground population but change the excited population, so AES is far more temperature-sensitive than AAS.
- Line widths: natural broadening from δE δt ≥ h/4π, lifetime about 10⁻⁸–10⁻⁹ s, width 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴ nm (δλ/λ = δE/ΔE); collisional (pressure) broadening, up to 10⁻³ nm for Na, grows with temperature; Doppler broadening grows with temperature. The lamp is cooler and at lower pressure than the flame, so its lines are narrower than the flame's absorption lines; monochromators cannot isolate lines narrower than 10⁻³–10⁻² nm.

## Chromatography deck and 4 Nov slides (extraction, chromatography basics)
- Extraction practice (4 Nov, slides 7–13): HA with K = 6.0, pK_a = 4.50, 40.0 mL aqueous 0.0250 M total acid, 20.0 mL organic. D = K[H⁺]/(K_a + [H⁺]) (acid) = K/(1 + 10^(pH−pK_a)); at pH 3.50 D = 5.45; f_aq = V_aq/(V_aq + D V_org) = 40/149 = 0.268 and 73.2% extracted; Cₙ = C₀ f^n; at pH 6.50 D = 0.0594, f_aq = 0.971, 2.88% extracted; one 40.0 mL extraction leaves 0.155 (84.5% extracted) while two 20.0 mL extractions leave 0.268² = 0.0720 (92.8% extracted): **several small portions beat one big one**; to get below 1.00×10⁻⁴ M from 0.0250 M, n > log(0.00400)/log(0.268) = 4.20, so **5 extractions**. For a weak base, lowering the pH leaves K unchanged but lowers D (the base becomes BH⁺, which stays in the water). Slide 3 says the original phase can be organic in the acid–base back-extraction case: check the f and D conventions in slides 3–6 (they are images).
- Chromatography basics: separation by different rates of movement between stationary and mobile phases (same principle as extraction, one phase fixed); eluent in, eluate out; the solute with more affinity for the mobile phase is detected first. Types by mobile phase (GC, LC), by bed (column, planar), by mechanism: adsorption (solid stationary phase, e.g. TLC), partition (liquid film; GC, HPLC, paper, reversed-phase), ion-exchange (fixed charged groups attract counter-ions), size exclusion (larger molecules elute first), affinity (most selective; antibody-bound column; release by changing pH or ionic strength). t_R = t_M + t_S; t_M is a property of the column and conditions, the same for every solute. Linear velocity u_x (cm/min) vs volume flow u_v (mL/min): open tube u_v = u_x π r², packed u_v = u_x π r² ε (ε = fraction of the column volume the stationary phase does not occupy); worked: 0.60 cm i.d., ε = 0.20, 0.30 mL/min → u_x = 5.3 cm/min. Scale-up: keep length and linear velocity; mass ∝ cross-sectional area, so mass ratio = (radius ratio)² and volume flow scales the same way: 2 mg on a 1.0 cm column → 20 mg needs radius 0.50 × √10 = 1.58 cm (diameter 3.16 cm) and 10× the flow (0.5 → 5 mL/min).
- k = t_S/t_M = (t_R − t_M)/t_M = (V_R − V_M)/V_M = n_S/n_M = c_S V_S/(c_M V_M) = K V_S/V_M; α = k_B/k_A = K_B/K_A (V_S/V_M cancels). Examples: butyric acid t_R 7.63 min, t_M 0.31 → k = 23.6; isobutyric 5.98 min → α = 7.32/5.67 = 1.29; methane 42 s, benzene 251 s, toluene 333 s → k 4.98 and 6.93, α = 1.39; K_A 4.0, K_B 8.0, V_S 5.0 mL, V_M 20.0 mL → k 1.0 and 2.0, α 2.0; open-tubular benzene k = 5.0, 250 µm i.d., 1.0 µm film: V_S/V_M = π(125² − 124²)/π·124² = 782 µm²/48305 µm² = 0.0162, K ≈ 309; toluene 200 s, t_M 40 s, 320 µm i.d., 2.0 µm film (slides 20–21, answer not extracted). Reversed-phase elution orders (4 Nov slides 31, 33): ethanol → 1-propanol → 1-butanol → 1-pentanol; acetone → 2-butanone → toluene → ethylbenzene (more hydrophobic = later).

Separation exploits size (filtration, size exclusion), volatility (distillation, GC), density (centrifugation), polarity/solubility (LLE, normal and reversed phase), charge (ion exchange, electrophoresis), affinity (affinity chromatography) (p16). ICP-MS quantifies metals at very low levels (p12).
- Solubility (p19–25): "like dissolves like"; forming a solution needs solvent–solvent and solute–solute interactions broken (cost energy) and solvent–solute interactions formed (release energy). Interaction types: dispersion (all molecules), dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding (H on N, O or F; strong and directional), ion–dipole (Na⁺ with water). Solubility depends on the overall balance.
- Solvent extraction (p18, p28): transfer of a solute between two immiscible liquids (usually aqueous and organic) because it dissolves differently in each; separatory funnel. K = concentration ratio at equilibrium (p29–30). Larger K = prefers the organic phase when K = C_org/C_aq. f₁ = V_aq/(V_aq + K V_org); after i equal extractions f = f₁^i; [S]_i = f [S]₀ (p33–39, p72–74). **Several small extractions beat one big one with the same total solvent** because each re-applies the equilibrium to a smaller amount (p41–46). Worked: K = 50/150 → f = 0.33 (50 mL aq, 25 mL org, K 4.0); K = 3, 100 mL: one 500 mL gives f = 0.062 (6%), five 100 mL give 0.25⁵ = 0.00098 (0.1%); iodine K = 85, 1 mM in 50 mL: 1×50 mL → 1.16×10⁻⁵ M, 2×25 mL → 5.28×10⁻⁷ M, 5×10 mL → 5.29×10⁻¹⁰ M; pesticide K = 20 between hexane (100 mL, original) and water (50 mL): f remaining in organic = V_org/(V_org + V_aq/K) = 0.976.
- Acid–base extraction (p50–65, p75–92): neutral form prefers organic, ionic form prefers water. Equilibria involved: ionisation in water (K_a) and partitioning of the neutral form (K); K describes the neutral species, D the overall distribution including ionisation. Acid: D = K[H⁺]/(K_a + [H⁺]) = K/(1 + 10^(pH−pK_a)); base: D = K·K_a/(K_a + [H⁺]) = K/(1 + 10^(pK_a−pH)) (K_a of BH⁺); with K_b: D = K[OH⁻]/([OH⁻] + K_b), K_a K_b = K_w. Trends: acid at high pH ionises and stays in water (D falls); base at low pH is protonated to BH⁺ and stays in water (D falls), at high pH D → K. R–NH₂ (neutral, organic) vs R–NH₃⁺ (ionic, aqueous). Worked: acid K 5.0, K_a 1.0×10⁻⁵: D = 4.55 at pH 4.00 and 0.455 at pH 6.00; base K 6.0, K_a 1.0×10⁻⁹: D ≈ 0.059 at pH 7.00; amine K 3, K_a 1×10⁻⁹, 50 mL organic (0.01 M) with 100 mL aqueous: pH 10 → D 2.73, f 0.58, 0.0058 M left in organic; pH 8 → D 0.273, f 0.12, 0.0044 M in the aqueous phase. HA practice (K 6.0, pK_a 4.50, 40 mL aqueous, 0.0250 M, 20 mL organic, Topic 4 combined p68–74 = 4 Nov slides 7–13): D 5.45, f_aq 0.268 (C 6.71×10⁻³ M), 73.2% extracted; pH 6.50: D 0.0594, 2.88% extracted; one 40 mL = 84.5% vs two 20 mL = 92.8%; n > 4.20 so 5 extractions to fall below 1.00×10⁻⁴ M.
- Extracting inorganic species (p93–94): hydrated free metal ions barely dissolve in non-polar solvents; chelating ligands (weak acids HL) bind metal ions to give neutral complexes that are soluble in organic solvents and insoluble in water; uses: separating rare earth and transition metals, hydrometallurgy, nuclear fuel reprocessing (U and Pu with TBP), pre-concentration before spectroscopy.
