=== stats | Statistics and uncertainty | t1-stats
summary: Mean and s, Q-test, confidence interval, the three t-tests, and error propagation, in the order the final asks them.

## Use it when
A question gives replicate data, asks you to reject a point, compare two methods, quote a confidence interval, or combine uncertainties.

## Steps
1. **Mean and s** (s uses N − 1). %RSD = $\dfrac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100$.
2. **Q-test** (90% only in the booklet): $Q = \dfrac{\text{gap}}{\text{range}}$, where gap is the suspect value to its nearest neighbour. Reject if Q_calc > Q_table (n = 4 0.76, 5 0.64, 6 0.56, 7 0.51, 8 0.47, 9 0.44, 10 0.41). If you reject, recompute the mean and s without it.
3. **Confidence interval:** $\bar{x} \pm t\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{N}}$, with t at df = N − 1 and the confidence level the question names.
4. **Pick the t-test.** Measured against a known value: $t = \dfrac{|\bar{x}-\mu|\sqrt{N}}{s}$, df = N − 1. Two methods on one sample, several replicates each (pooled): $s_{pooled} = \sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2(N_1-1)+s_2^2(N_2-1)}{N_1+N_2-2}}$, $t = \dfrac{|\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2|}{s_{pooled}}\sqrt{\dfrac{N_1N_2}{N_1+N_2}}$, df = $N_1+N_2-2$. Two methods on several different samples (paired): take each difference d, then $t = \dfrac{|\bar{d}|\sqrt{n}}{s_d}$, df = n − 1.
5. **Decide in words:** if t_cal > t_table the results differ significantly at that confidence level; otherwise they do not. Say which, and quote both numbers.
6. **Booklet table rule:** the t table lists df 1–10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 60, 120. If your df is not listed, read the next **lower** row (11 df → row 10; 26 df → row 25). The recent finals use 99.9%.
7. **Propagation:** add or subtract → absolute errors combine as √(e₁² + e₂²). Multiply or divide → percent errors combine as √(%e₁² + %e₂²). Mixed: do the bracket with absolute errors first, convert to a percent, then combine.

## Worked mini-example
$\dfrac{1.76(\pm0.03)-0.59(\pm0.02)}{1.89(\pm0.02)}$: the bracket is 1.17 ± √(0.03² + 0.02²) = 1.17 ± 0.036, that is 3.1%. Then 3.1% and 0.02/1.89 = 1.1% give √(3.1² + 1.1²) = 3.3%. The answer is 0.619 ± 3%, or ± 0.020. Keep every digit until the end.

## Traps
- Using the 95% column when the question says 99% or 99.9%.
- Using the wrong df: pooled is N₁ + N₂ − 2, paired is n − 1.
- Forgetting √N in the t formula.
- Applying the pooled test to paired data (or the reverse).
- Combining percent errors for an addition, or absolute errors for a multiplication.
- Using a different Q table than the 90% one in the booklet.

=== psi | PSI and air quality | a-psi
summary: Sub-index by interpolation, overall PSI, and the breathing and cigarette chain.

## Use it when
You get a 24-hour pollutant concentration and must give a PSI, a descriptor, or an equivalent number of cigarettes.

## Steps
1. Find the band that holds your concentration in the booklet table.
2. **Interpolate:** $I = \dfrac{\text{PSI}_{upper}-\text{PSI}_{lower}}{C_{upper}-C_{lower}}\,(C-C_{lower}) + \text{PSI}_{lower}$. The lower anchor is the **top of the band below** (the booklet's I_max,J). For the lowest band use 0 and 0.
3. **Overall PSI = the largest sub-index**; report that pollutant.
4. **Descriptor:** 0–50 good, 51–100 moderate, 101–200 unhealthy, 201–300 very unhealthy, above 300 hazardous.
5. **Air breathed:** rate (L/min) × 60 × 24 / 1000 = m³ per day. At 8 L/min that is 11.52 m³.
6. **Mass inhaled** = concentration (µg m⁻³) × volume. One cigarette is taken as 12 mg = 12 000 µg of PM2.5. Cigarettes = mass / 12 mg.
7. **24-hour versus 1-hour:** the 24-hour value averages the present hour and the previous 23, so it moves slowly. The 1-hour value tracks sudden haze or fires.

## Worked mini-example
PM2.5 = 70 µg m⁻³ lies between 55 (PSI 100) and 150 (PSI 200): $I = \dfrac{100}{95}(70-55)+100 = 116$, unhealthy. A year at 40 µg m⁻³: 11.52 × 365 = 4205 m³, × 40 = 168 mg, which is 14 cigarettes.

## Traps
- Using the bottom of the band as the lower anchor.
- A straight ratio from zero (only right inside the lowest band).
- Averaging the sub-indices instead of taking the largest.
- Forgetting the ×365, or mixing µg and mg.
- Why the descriptors can mislead: extrapolated health data, no error bars, composition ignored (cigarette tar versus haze metals), no time dimension.

=== quant | Units, dilutions and Kjeldahl | t1-quant
summary: ppm and molarity, dilution chains, Kjeldahl by back-titration, and the melamine ppm.

## Use it when
A question converts concentration units, undoes a dilution, or goes from a titration to % nitrogen.

## Steps
1. **ppm** in dilute water = mg per litre. Molarity = $\dfrac{\text{ppm}/1000\ \text{g L}^{-1}}{M_r}$. 1 ppm = 1000 ppb.
2. **Dilution factor** = final volume ÷ volume taken. For a chain multiply the factors, then **multiply the measured concentration by the factor** to get back to the original.
3. **Kjeldahl by back-titration:** mmol N = mmol acid added − mmol base used (1 : 1 : 1). Mass N = mmol × 14.01 mg. %N = mass N ÷ sample mass × 100. % protein = %N × the stated factor.
4. **Melamine** (C₃H₆N₆, M = 126.15, N = 84.06): $\text{melamine mass} = \text{N mass} \times \dfrac{126.15}{84.06}$. ppm = µg per g.

## Worked mini-example
25.00 mL made up to 250.0 mL, then 10.00 mL of that to 100.0 mL gives DF = 10 × 10 = 100. A reading of 4.0 ppm in the final solution means 400 ppm in the original.

## Traps
- Dividing by the dilution factor instead of multiplying.
- Undoing only one step of a two-step dilution.
- Forgetting that the acid you add is mostly used up by ammonia: subtract the back-titration.
- Using the molar mass of the compound where the nitrogen mass is needed.

=== nernst | Ionic strength, activity and cells | e-activity,e-nernst
summary: I and γ, one-electron half-cells, E_cell, spontaneity, ΔG and K, E° from Ksp.

## Use it when
A question asks for an activity, a half-cell or cell potential with activities, spontaneity, ΔG°, or E° from a solubility product.

## Steps
1. **Ionic strength** I = ½Σcz². Shortcuts for the salt's I/c: 1:1 → c, 1:2 → 3c, 2:2 → 4c, 1:3 → 6c.
2. **Activity coefficient:** look γ up in the booklet table (nearest I column, the ion's size α), or use $-\log\gamma = \dfrac{0.51\,z^2\sqrt{I}}{1+3.3\,\alpha\sqrt{I}}$ with α in nm. a = γ·c.
3. **Half-reactions as one-electron reductions** (write ½Cu²⁺ + e⁻ → ½Cu). E = E° − 0.0592 log(Q) with Q built from **activities**; solids have a = 1.
4. **Cell:** E_cell = E_right − E_left, with the **more positive** reduction potential on the right.
5. **Spontaneous** if E_cell > 0 (ΔG < 0).
6. ΔG° = −nFE°_cell, with n of the reaction as written. K from $\log K = \dfrac{nE^\circ}{0.0592}$.
7. **E° from Ksp:** $E^\circ_{\text{salt}} = E^\circ_{\text{M}^+/\text{M}} + \dfrac{0.0592}{n}\log K_{sp}$, with n the electrons in the salt's half-reaction (Ag₂SO₃ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag + SO₃²⁻ has n = 2: E° = 0.799 + 0.0296 log Ksp).
8. **Reference electrodes:** SCE → SHE add 0.242 V, SHE → SCE subtract it.

## Worked mini-example
Ag₂SO₃ with Ksp = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁴: E° = 0.799 + 0.0296 × (−13.82) = +0.390 V. Mixing LaCl₃ (0.05 M, I = 6c = 0.30 M) with 3 mol eq NaIO₃ precipitates La(IO₃)₃ and leaves 0.15 M NaCl, I = 0.15 M: ionic strength falls.

## Traps
- Using concentration in the log term when activity is asked.
- Writing a two-electron half-reaction when the course convention is one electron (it changes ΔG, not E).
- Swapping E_right and E_left.
- Forgetting that solids are 1.
- Using the 0.0592 slope for a different temperature.

=== electrodes | How the electrodes work (written answers) | p-principles,p-types
summary: The standard answer skeletons for ISE cells, junction potential, membranes, gas probes and TISAB.

## Use it when
A question says draw, label, explain, or why. Marks are one point per fact, so state each fact in its own sentence.

## Steps
1. **Cell notation:** reference electrode on the left, sensor on the right. E_cell = E_right − E_left = E_G + E_G′ + E_R′ − (E_R + E_J), where only E_G changes with the ion.
2. **Junction potential:** ions diffuse at different rates (H⁺ faster than Cl⁻), so charge builds up across the junction. It is the largest error. Keep it constant by giving standards and sample the same ionic strength and temperature.
3. **Why concentration is enough:** an ISE responds to activity. A constant background (ISA or TISAB) fixes γ, so the signal follows concentration.
4. **Membranes:** glass (conditioned in 0.1 M HCl, a(H⁺), alkali ions interfere), LaF₃ (F⁻ hops between vacancies, OH⁻ interferes), Ag₂S (senses S²⁻ through Ksp), PVC with an ionophore (valinomycin for K⁺).
5. **Gas probes:** gas-permeable membrane over an inner solution with an ISE; NH₃ with 0.1 M NH₄Cl and a pH glass electrode, E = const − 59.2 log[NH₃].
6. **TISAB for fluoride:** NaCl for ionic strength, acetate buffer (pH 5–6, avoids OH⁻), CDTA to free F⁻ from Al(III) and Fe(III).
7. **NH₄NO₃ bridge** keeps K⁺ from the saturated KCl reference out of the sample.

## Traps
- Saying "the electrode measures concentration" without the constant-γ reason.
- Forgetting that the junction potential is constant only if the two solutions match.
- For the NH₃ probe, forgetting that the slope is negative.
- A list of words with no mechanism: markers give a mark per mechanism, not per keyword.

=== calibration | ISE calibration and the dilution chain | p-calibration
summary: Slope, reading an unknown, undoing the dilutions, and weight % N with the ammonia probe.

## Use it when
You get standards (E against log c) and a sample reading, usually after one or two dilutions.

## Steps
1. **Slope** = ΔE per decade. Two standards n decades apart: $\dfrac{E_{high}-E_{low}}{n}$. Theory at 25 °C: ±59.2/z mV, positive for cations, negative for anions and the NH₃ probe. Nernstian if within about ±2 mV.
2. **log c of the sample** = $\log c_{ref} + \dfrac{E-E_{ref}}{\text{slope}}$.
3. c = 10^(log c).
4. **Undo each dilution:** multiply by $\dfrac{V_{final}}{V_{taken}}$ for every step.
5. **To a mass:** n = c × V (litres) of the solution you have just reached; mass = n × molar mass.
6. **To weight %:** mass of the analyte ÷ sample mass × 100. For N from NH₃ the ratio is 1 : 1 and M = 14.01.
7. State the assumption: constant ionic strength so concentration stands in for activity, and the reading is in the linear range (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹ M).

## Worked mini-example
Standards at 10⁻⁴ M and 10⁻² M read 400.0 and 282.0 mV: slope = $\dfrac{282.0-400.0}{2} = -59.0$. A diluted sample reads 303.7 mV: $\log c = -4 + \dfrac{303.7-400.0}{-59.0} = -2.37$, so 4.3 × 10⁻³ M. If 10 mL was diluted to 100 mL, the digest holds 4.3 × 10⁻² M; in 250 mL that is 10.7 mmol N, 150 mg.

## Traps
- Dropping the negative sign of a gas-probe slope.
- Dividing by the number of standards instead of the number of decades.
- Forgetting a dilution step, or dividing by the factor.
- Using NH₃ (17.04) instead of N (14.01).
- Reading a value outside the standards without saying so.

=== selectivity | Interferents and selectivity | p-selectivity
summary: The two-solution selectivity coefficient, step by step, and how to classify an interferent.

## Use it when
You get E₁ (primary ion alone) and E₂ (primary ion plus interferent) and must find K^pot.

## Steps
1. Work out the **ionic strength of each solution** (the interferent salt dominates the second).
2. **γ for each ion** at the right I (extended Debye–Hückel with the α values given, or the table).
3. **Activities:** a_i in the pure solution, a_i′ in the mixture (different I, so different γ), a_j for the interferent in the mixture.
4. **$K = \dfrac{a_i\,10^{(E_2-E_1)/s}-a_i'}{a_j^{\,n/x}}$** with n, x the charges of primary and interfering ion, s the slope.
5. **Classify:** below 10⁻³ very weak, 10⁻³ to 1 intermediate, above 1 the electrode prefers the interferent (the papers say weak, intermediate, strong).
6. **Interference error:** the interferent's contribution as an equivalent activity is K·a_j^(n/x).

## Worked mini-example
Ca²⁺ electrode (s = 29.6 mV): 0.001 M CaCl₂ (I = 0.003, γ = 0.79, a_i = 7.9 × 10⁻⁴); with 0.1 M NaCl (I = 0.103, γ(Ca) = 0.40, γ(Na) = 0.77, a_i′ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴, a_j = 7.7 × 10⁻²). With E₂ − E₁ = +0.3 mV the result is K ≈ 6.9 × 10⁻²: intermediate.

## Traps
- Using the same γ in both solutions.
- Mixing up the exponent n/x (primary over interferent).
- Keeping too few significant figures: the two numerator terms nearly cancel.
- Calling 0.3 to 1 "strong": strong means above 1.

=== absorption | Absorption spectroscopy | s-absorption
summary: Beer–Lambert, %T, dilution chains, molar mass from absorbance, and wavenumber and energy conversions.

## Use it when
A question has A, %T, ε, a path length, a dilution, or a unit conversion for light.

## Steps
1. **T = %T/100.** A = −log T = εbc, with b in cm, c in M, ε in M⁻¹ cm⁻¹.
2. Solve for the unknown; keep the units consistent.
3. **Undo the dilutions** (multiply by $\dfrac{V_{final}}{V_{taken}}$ for each step).
4. **Molar mass** = mass dissolved ÷ (c_original × volume).
5. **Conversions:** $\tilde\nu = \dfrac{10^7}{\lambda}$ (λ in nm, ν̃ in cm⁻¹); 1 nm = 10 Å; $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ with h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
6. **Deviations from Beer's law:** high concentration, association or dissociation of the absorber, non-monochromatic or stray light.

## Worked mini-example
T = 44.1%, b = 2.10 cm, c = 7.25 × 10⁻⁵ M: A = −log 0.441 = 0.356, ε = 0.356/(2.10 × 7.25 × 10⁻⁵) = 2.34 × 10³ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹.

## Traps
- Using %T where T is needed.
- A log of the wrong sign (A is positive).
- Forgetting a dilution factor.
- State the assumption when asked: only the analyte absorbs at that wavelength.

=== fluorescence | Fluorescence | s-fluorescence
summary: Label the Jablonski diagram, explain the sensitivity and the Stokes shift, and the two-point calibration.

## Use it when
A question labels processes, asks why fluorescence is sensitive, draws the instrument, or reads a concentration from a calibration.

## Steps
1. **Jablonski labels:** A absorption; R vibrational relaxation (to the lowest vibrational level, very fast); IC internal conversion (same spin, between electronic states); ISC intersystem crossing (S₁ → T₁, spin changes); F fluorescence (S₁ → S₀, same spin, fast); P phosphorescence (T₁ → S₀, spin-forbidden, slow, 10⁻⁴ to 10² s).
2. **Stokes shift:** emission is at longer wavelength than excitation because of geometry re-optimisation in S₁ and solvent interactions.
3. **Sensitivity:** the detector sits at 90° to the beam and sees only emitted light on a near-zero background; absorption measures a small difference between two large signals.
4. **Instrument:** source, excitation monochromator, sample cell, emission monochromator, detector at 90°.
5. **Quenching:** M* + Q → M + Q*, and Q* loses heat. It lowers the signal, and temperature rises lower the quantum yield.
6. **Two-point calibration:** slope = $\dfrac{\Delta I}{\Delta c}$, then $c = \dfrac{I-\text{intercept}}{\text{slope}}$.
7. **Who fluoresces:** rigid, conjugated molecules (less internal conversion).

## Traps
- Putting the detector in line with the beam.
- Confusing phosphorescence (spin change, slow) with fluorescence.
- Saying the Stokes shift comes from "energy loss to heat" with no cause.
- Atomic emission has no Stokes shift: do not apply this page to AES.

=== atomic | Atomic spectroscopy | s-atomic
summary: AAS layout, Boltzmann populations, temperature sensitivity, line widths, and ICP versus AAS.

## Use it when
A question asks for the AAS components, an excited-state fraction, a comparison of AES with AAS, or why atomic lines are narrow.

## Steps
1. **AAS order:** hollow-cathode lamp, atomiser (nebuliser and flame), monochromator, detector. The monochromator isolates the lamp line from flame emission.
2. **Hollow-cathode lamp:** Ar⁺ sputters metal atoms from the cathode, which are excited and emit the line the analyte absorbs (lines narrower than 10⁻³ to 10⁻² nm).
3. **Boltzmann:** $\dfrac{N_e}{N_0} = \dfrac{g_e}{g_0}\exp\left[-\dfrac{E_e-E_0}{kT}\right]$ with k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹. Compute it at both temperatures and compare.
4. **AES is more temperature-sensitive than AAS** because the excited fraction changes strongly with T while the ground-state population (over 99.98%) barely does.
5. **Line widths:** natural (Heisenberg, δE·δt ≥ h/4π), collisional (pressure) and Doppler (temperature) broadening. The lamp is cooler and at lower pressure than the flame, so its lines are narrower than the absorption lines.
6. **ICP versus AAS:** hotter plasma, complete atomisation, many elements at once, less oxide formation.

## Worked mini-example
Na with E_e = 3.371 × 10⁻¹⁹ J and g_e/g₀ = 2: N_e/N₀ = 1.67 × 10⁻⁴ at 2600 K and 1.74 × 10⁻⁴ at 2610 K, a 4% rise for a 10 K change.

## Traps
- Using the booklet's h instead of k in the exponent.
- Forgetting the g_e/g₀ factor.
- Giving the AES wavelength a Stokes shift (it has none).

=== chromcalc | Retention, resolution and plates | c-calc
summary: k and α, R_S from times and widths, N and H, Purnell, and the flow and scale-up calculations.

## Use it when
A question gives retention times, widths, a column length, or asks for plates or length needed.

## Steps
1. **Retention factor** $k = \dfrac{t_R-t_M}{t_M}$. **Selectivity** $\alpha = \dfrac{k_B}{k_A}$ (B the later peak). From K: $k = K\,\dfrac{V_S}{V_M}$.
2. **Resolution:** $R_S = \dfrac{2(t_B-t_A)}{W_A+W_B}$ with baseline widths, or $R_S = \dfrac{1.178(t_B-t_A)}{w_{1/2,A}+w_{1/2,B}}$ with half-height widths (the two constants are not interchangeable). Below 1.0 poor, about 1.0 partial, 1.5 or more baseline.
3. **Plates:** $N = 16\left(\dfrac{t_R}{W}\right)^2$ (baseline width) or $N = 5.55\left(\dfrac{t_R}{w_{1/2}}\right)^2$. $H = \dfrac{L}{N}$.
4. **Purnell:** $R_S = \dfrac{\sqrt{N}}{4}\,\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}\,\dfrac{k_B}{1+k_B}$. Solve for N: $N = 16R_S^2\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)^2\left(\dfrac{1+k_B}{k_B}\right)^2$. Then L = N × H.
5. **Length scaling:** $R_S \propto \sqrt{L}$, so doubling $R_S$ needs four times the length.
6. **Flow:** packed column $u_v = u_x\,\pi r^2\,\varepsilon$. Scale-up keeps length and u_x; sample mass and flow scale with the area (r²).
7. **Open tube:** $\dfrac{V_M}{V_S}\approx\dfrac{d_c}{4d_f}$, then $K = k\,\dfrac{V_M}{V_S}$.

## Worked mini-example
Peaks at 7.20 and 8.10 min, W = 0.48 and 0.60: $R_S = \dfrac{2(0.90)}{1.08} = 1.67$. Both widths +25%: R_S = 1.67/1.25 = 1.33.

## Traps
- The factor 2 missing in R_S, or using 1.178 with baseline widths.
- Using 16 with a half-height width.
- k as t_R/t_M.
- Treating R_S as proportional to L.
- Dropping a squared term in the Purnell rearrangement.

=== chromconcepts | Band spreading, peak shape and types | c-concepts
summary: The van Deemter terms, which dominates where, tailing and fronting, and the types of chromatography.

## Use it when
A question asks which term dominates, what causes a peak shape, or which mechanism a method uses.

## Steps
1. **$H = A + \dfrac{B}{u} + Cu$.** A: multiple flow paths (zero in an open tube). B/u: longitudinal diffusion (dominates at low flow, larger in GC). Cu: slow equilibration between phases (dominates at high flow).
2. **At the optimum** velocity B/u = Cu, H is smallest and N largest.
3. **Reduce each term:** small, uniform, well-packed particles for A; faster flow for B/u; slower flow, smaller particles, thin films for Cu.
4. **Tailing:** some molecules held too strongly (silanols, active sites); cure by end-capping, a different pH or mobile phase, or a smaller injection. **Fronting:** overload. A/B > 1 is tailing in the lecture convention.
5. **Types:** adsorption (solid, TLC), partition (liquid film), ion exchange (fixed charges, counter-ions), size exclusion (large elute first), affinity (specific binder).
6. **Void time t_M** is the same for every solute. The solute with more affinity for the mobile phase leaves first.

## Traps
- Saying longitudinal diffusion is equal in GC and LC.
- Putting the optimum where B/u dominates.
- Saying a more hydrophobic solute has a longer t_M.
- The direction of the isotherm effect on tailing is disputed between the decks: do not argue it, use the causes above.

=== extraction | Extraction | x-calc,x-concepts
summary: D at a pH, which formula for the fraction left, repeated portions, and the written reasons.

## Use it when
A solute is split between water and an organic solvent, with a pH, a K, volumes and a target concentration.

## Steps
1. **Which phase does it start in?** Acid–base problems in the exam start in the **organic** phase; plain K problems start in water.
2. **Distribution coefficient:** acid $D = \dfrac{K}{1+K_a/[\text{H}^+]}$; base $D = \dfrac{K}{1+[\text{H}^+]/K_a}$ (K_a of BH⁺). At pH = pK_a an acid has $D = \dfrac{K}{2}$.
3. **Fraction left after one extraction:** starting in the organic phase $f = \dfrac{V_{org}}{V_{org}+V_{aq}/D}$; starting in water $f = \dfrac{V_{aq}}{V_{aq}+D\,V_{org}}$. With plain K use K in place of D.
4. **Concentration left** = c₀·f. **% extracted** = 100(1 − f).
5. **n portions:** $c = c_0 f^n$, so $n = \dfrac{\ln(c/c_0)}{\ln f}$, **rounded up**. Total volume = n × the portion volume.
6. **Which pH leaves more behind?** An acid ionises at high pH and stays in water, so the organic solvent keeps more at the lower pH. A base is the reverse.
7. **Several small portions beat one large one** with the same total solvent.

## Worked mini-example
K = 4.1, K_a = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵, pH 5 ([H⁺] = 10⁻⁵): $D = \dfrac{4.1}{1+6.3} = 0.562$. With 25 mL ether and 100 mL water, $f = \dfrac{25}{25+100/0.562} = 0.123$, so 0.15 M becomes 1.85 × 10⁻² M, and 87.7% is extracted.

## Traps
- Using the water-start formula for an acid that starts in the organic phase.
- Using K where D is needed at a given pH.
- Not rounding the number of portions up.
- Using the first volume for the repeated extractions.
- Mixing up which pH is higher when you explain.

=== gc | GC: columns, programming and detectors | g-gc,g-detect
summary: Answer skeletons for the descriptive GC parts, one point per fact.

## Use it when
The question asks you to compare, explain or choose, and marks are one per point.

## Steps
1. **Open tubular versus packed:** open tubular gives higher resolution, shorter analysis, higher sensitivity and lower sample capacity; packed gives higher capacity, broader peaks and longer retention. Applications for capillary columns: complex mixtures (petrol, essential oils, environmental trace analysis).
2. **Temperature programming:** a ramp lets a mixture of boiling points elute in one run, sharpening late peaks and shortening the time; pressure programming does the same without heat.
3. **Injection:** split (high concentration, avoids overload), splitless (trace, vent opened later), on-column (thermally labile or high boiling).
4. **Carrier gas:** He, N₂ or H₂; H₂ and He allow faster flow; H₂ is flammable.
5. **Detectors:** TCD (heated filament in He; an analyte changes the thermal conductivity, filament temperature and resistance; universal, non-destructive, less sensitive); FID (H₂–air flame, ions from carbon compounds, very sensitive, destroys the sample); MS (m/z, identifies).
6. **GC–MS versus LC–MS:** MS needs gas-phase ions under vacuum, so the GC effluent is already a gas, while LC must first be desolvated (ESI, APCI).

## Traps
- Giving a detector name without its mechanism.
- Calling TCD destructive.
- "Better resolution" without saying why (an open column has no packing, so A = 0).

=== hplc | HPLC: columns, modes and elution order | g-hplc
summary: Column label, normal versus reversed phase, elution order, isocratic versus gradient, detectors.

## Use it when
You must order solutes, explain a mode, or choose a method.

## Steps
1. **Column label:** inner diameter × length (3.9 × 150 mm). C18 and C8 are alkyl chains bonded to silica; C8 retains less. Silica degrades at high pH.
2. **Normal phase:** polar stationary phase, non-polar mobile phase, **least polar elutes first**. **Reversed phase:** non-polar stationary phase, polar mobile phase, **most polar elutes first**.
3. **Order the solutes:** for reversed phase rank by polarity, most polar first (more hydrophobic, longer chain, heavier halogen elutes later). Write the full order.
4. **Raising the organic fraction** makes the mobile phase stronger and shortens retention.
5. **Isocratic** keeps the composition fixed; **gradient** changes it during the run, which suits mixtures of weakly and strongly retained solutes.
6. **Detectors:** UV (most common, diode array), refractive index (universal, about 1000× less sensitive), electrochemical (electroactive compounds only).
7. **Smaller particles** give higher N but need higher pressure.
8. **GC or HPLC?** GC for volatile, thermally stable compounds; HPLC for polar, non-volatile or thermally labile ones.

## Traps
- Reversing normal and reversed phase.
- Thinking more organic solvent in reversed phase slows elution.
- A gradient advantage with no reason attached.
